# Referees — every axis, measured

Generated by `ml/referee_report.py`. Pre-registrations:
[ADR 0007](../docs/adr/0007-referee-analysis-axes-are-pre-registered.md) for axes A/B/C, `ml/referee_player_preregistration.md` (and its 2026-08-21 amendment) for D, E and F.

**Corpus.** 13,114 regular-season games with three named officials, a play stream and a box score; 511,534 foul plays; 268,023 player-games. Seasons 2015-16 … 2025-26. The postseason was added on 2026-08-21 — **913 playoff games** over the same seasons, fetched by `scripts/fetch_playoff_officials.ts`, and reported in its own section below. Every regular-season figure above and below excludes it.

*Correction, 2026-08-21.* An earlier run filtered on exactly three listed officials, which silently dropped the 295 regular-season and 309 playoff games where ESPN also lists a standby fourth. The filter is now `>= 3` and the working crew is the first three by `order`; every figure here is the corrected run.

Every test asks the same question first: does the official dimension carry more variance than a permutation null in which each official's games are a random draw from the same seasons? That global test is multiplicity-free, and under the pre-registration **no named figure may be published from an axis whose global test does not clear p < 0.01**.

Three officials work every game and the play stream never records which one blew the whistle, so every per-official figure below is roughly **a third** of the effect it names.

The lead-size gradient, which is the largest effect anywhere in this report:

| lead | fouls called on the team that is ahead |
|---|---|
| 1-5 points | 49.61% (192,677 fouls) |
| 6-10 points | 51.29% (131,598 fouls) |
| 11-20 points | 54.11% (122,792 fouls) |
| 21+ points | 58.34% (40,689 fouls) |

**This is not evidence of a whistle.** A trailing team attacks the rim and a leading team protects its lead, so the foul follows the basketball. The gradient is reported because it is large and real, not because it is a referee effect.

## The playoffs, where the legends actually live

The five pairs were named in the pre-registration and committed **before `scripts/fetch_playoff_officials.ts` existed**, so the claim list could not have been chosen with any sight of these numbers. Expectation here is opponent-aware: a win model fitted on all 1,826 playoff team-games from each side's regular-season strength and home court, because senior officials draw later rounds against better teams and that alone would manufacture losing records.

| claim | playoff record | expected | difference | p | opponent strength with / without |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scott Foster × Chris Paul | 1–10 | 6.34 W | -5.34 W | 0.0006 | 0.599 / 0.606 |
| Scott Foster × James Harden | 5–11 | 7.69 W | -2.69 W | 0.1246 | 0.677 / 0.644 |
| Tony Brothers × Luka Doncic | 4–5 | 3.69 W | +0.31 W | 0.7141 | 0.690 / 0.678 |
| Tony Brothers × Kevin Durant | 7–3 | 4.76 W | +2.24 W | 0.9686 | 0.682 / 0.655 |
| Marc Davis × Russell Westbrook | 5–8 | 5.34 W | -0.34 W | 0.5399 | 0.678 / 0.682 |

Four of the five are nothing, and Brothers × Durant runs the *opposite* way. The fifth is not nothing.

**Chris Paul's teams went 1–10 in playoff games Scott Foster worked**, against 6.34 expected. His games with Foster were not harder than his other playoff games (opponent strength 0.599 against 0.606), so the assignment explanation does not cover it. Across every playoff pair with at least 10 shared games it ranks **#1 of 689** — the single most lopsided official-and-player record in eleven postseasons.

**And that is exactly the problem.** Somebody has to be first:

- The most extreme p-value 689 pairs of pure noise would produce is **0.00145**. Foster × Paul comes in at **0.0016** — barely past what the maximum of nothing looks like.
- 7 pairs clear p < 0.01. Chance predicts 6.9.
- 27 clear p < 0.05. Chance predicts 34.5 — there are *fewer* than there should be.

Scott Foster appears five times in that list and **four of them are teams winning** — Miles McBride, Josh Hart, OG Anunoby and Mitchell Robinson all went roughly ten-and-one with him. The same official is a curse for one star and a charm for four others, which is what a large grid of coin flips looks like from close up.

**The one test that would settle it cannot be run.** The claim was in circulation by 2019-20. Before that split Paul was 0–6 with Foster; after it, 1–4. The direction holds, but both halves fall under the pre-registered minimum of 10 games, so **neither is testable** and no verdict is drawn from either. A claim the public found by looking can only be confirmed by games nobody had seen when they found it, and there are five of those.

## Make-up calls: a t = 27 result, killed by its own mechanism test

Consecutive fouls switch teams **52.39%** of the time against **50.47%** from a shuffle that holds each team's foul total fixed — an excess of +1.93 points at t=+27.4, rising to +2.34 points for calls under 120 seconds apart. On its own that reads as compensation.

It is not. That null destroys the possession structure, and basketball alternates possessions. The test that separates the two uses the case where they predict **opposite signs**: an offensive foul is a turnover, so the fouling team goes straight onto defense and mechanics predicts the next foul lands on *them* — below chance — while compensation still predicts above.

| first foul | next foul switches teams | null | excess | t |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| defensive (ball changes hands) | 53.01% | 50.46% | +2.55 pp | +33.6 |
| **offensive (turnover)** | 48.53% | 50.48% | -1.95 pp | -6.8 |
| offensive, within 15 seconds | 21.96% | 50.50% | -28.54 pp | -45.6 |

The sign flips exactly where possession says it should, and within fifteen seconds of an offensive foul the next whistle goes against the **same team 78% of the time**. **The alternation is the ball changing hands, not the referee evening things up.**

The excess by gap, which traces the possession clock rather than any human intention:

| gap between calls | excess over the shuffled null |
|---|---|
| 0-15s | +2.61 pp (t=+14.6) |
| 15-30s | +5.85 pp (t=+32.7) |
| 30-60s | +1.27 pp (t=+8.7) |
| 60-120s | +1.01 pp (t=+7.1) |
| 120-300s | +0.35 pp (t=+2.0) |
| 300-10000s | -0.28 pp (t=-0.5) |

## Does the home whistle need a crowd?

2020-21 is the experiment: one season, one rulebook, one set of officials, with and without an audience.

| games | n | away share of fouls | away − home |
|---|---|---|---|
| no attendance field | 560 | 0.4998 | +0.030 |
| attendance reported | 483 | 0.5086 | +0.667 |
| crowd < 3,000 | 224 | 0.5091 | +0.723 |
| crowd >= 3,000 | 259 | 0.5082 | +0.618 |
| 2019-20 restart, no crowd | 88 | 0.5035 | +0.284 |
| 2019-20 same season, crowds | 971 | 0.5047 | +0.379 |
| every game, all seasons | 13,114 | 0.5042 | +0.338 |

One comparison suggests the whistle needs an audience and two do not. The 2019-20 restart shows no gap at all, and across all 12,440 games with an attendance figure, doubling the crowd moves the away foul share by +0.243 points at t=+1.09. **Read as a null**, with the caveat that a missing attendance field is being treated as an empty building, which is an inference rather than a fact.
